In hacker slang, "wetware" is the part of a system that isn't hardware or software: the person. This page is about mine. It covers how I train, eat, sleep and protect my attention, and why I do it this way.

doctrine

I think of my body and mind the way I think of a machine I maintain. If they run badly, everything else I do runs badly too. So instead of waiting for motivation, I set up a few simple routines and keep them running.

Everything here is a minimum, not a goal. Each section describes the least I do in a normal week, the part that should happen even when work is busy or plans fall apart. Anything on top of that is a bonus.

body

My main sport is Muay Thai, the Thai style of kickboxing. Everything else in my training is there to make me better at it: strength work is meant to make me hit harder and last longer, not to look a certain way.

Fight training depends on a gym, a class schedule and getting there. So underneath it I keep a small routine I can do at home with nothing but a kettlebell and my own bodyweight: a couple of short sessions a week, no travel, nothing to book. That is my minimum. If I also make it to the gym, great, but the gym never counts toward the minimum.

Through the day I also do a few easy sets, never close to my limit, just to keep moving.

The test I use: if something can be cancelled by someone else's schedule, it can't be part of the minimum.

fuel

I eat a little more than I burn, so I can build muscle slowly without putting on much fat. Protein comes first in every meal, and most of what I eat is cooked at home.

For supplements I keep to creatine and the usual basics. Nothing unusual.

I don't count calories forever. I track what I eat for a while, long enough to see what I really eat instead of what I think I eat, and then I stop.

recovery

I go to bed in the same window and wake up at the same time every day, weekends included. Keeping the times fixed matters more to me than hitting an exact number of hours. Once the schedule is steady, the sleep tends to sort itself out.

Mornings start with a few minutes of breathing practice: breathing from the belly, holding my breath to get used to higher levels of CO2, and a short time sitting still. I judge it by whether my breath holds get longer, not by how calm I feel afterwards.

attention

This is the part most people skip, and I think it decides whether the rest works. Training and sleep don't help much if my attention belongs to my phone. Two rules:

  • When I study, my phone is in another room.
  • I only scroll feeds at set times of the day, never in between.

What I study in those sessions is on the roadmap.

cadence

I plan in blocks of twelve weeks. I add one new habit at a time and only start the next once the last one happens without thinking. If a habit fails twice, I take that as a sign it was too big, and I split it into smaller steps.

The whole routine has to be simple enough to write on a single card from memory. If I can't, it has grown too complicated, and something gets cut.

During a block the plan doesn't change. When I come across a new idea or a better system, I write it down and look at it at the end of the block, not in the middle. Rebuilding everything each time something interesting shows up is exactly the habit this is meant to stop.
