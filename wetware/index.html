Wetware

How I train, eat, sleep and protect my focus, and why I do it that way.

In hacker slang, "wetware" is the part of a system that isn't hardware or software: the person. This page is about mine. It covers how I train, eat, sleep and protect my attention, and why I do it this way.

doctrine

I treat my body and mind as infrastructure I program myself. If they run badly, everything else I do runs badly too. So instead of waiting for motivation, I set up a few simple routines and keep them running.

Everything here is a minimum, not a goal. Each section describes the least I do in a normal week, the part that should happen even when work is busy or plans fall apart. Anything on top of that is a bonus.

body

My main sport is Muay Thai, the Thai style of kickboxing. Everything else in my training is there to make me better at it: strength work is meant to make me hit harder and last longer, not to look a certain way. Twice a week at a gym, and maybe one day an amateur fight.

Fight training depends on a gym, a class schedule and getting there. So underneath it I keep a routine I can do at home with a kettlebell, a pull-up bar and a mat. Two short sessions a week, alternating:

session agoblet squat, push-up, suitcase carry, hollow hold
session bkettlebell romanian deadlift, single-arm row, dead hang, scapular pull-up
every daya few sets of push-ups, dead hangs and a deep squat hold, never close to failure

That is my minimum. If I also make it to the gym, great, but the gym never counts toward it. The test I use: if something can be cancelled by someone else's schedule, it can't be part of the minimum.

Starting point, October 2026: 22 push-ups in one set, cold.

fuel

I eat 300 to 400 kcal more than I burn, so I can build muscle slowly without putting on much fat, and aim for 105 to 125 g of protein a day. Protein comes first in every meal, and most of what I eat is cooked at home.

Supplements: creatine (5 g a day), vitamin D3 with K2, omega-3 and magnesium. Nothing unusual.

I don't count calories forever. I weigh myself and track what I eat for a while, long enough to see what I really eat instead of what I think I eat, and then I stop.

recovery

I go to bed in the same window and wake up at the same time every day, weekends included. Keeping the times fixed matters more to me than hitting an exact number of hours. Once the schedule is steady, the sleep tends to sort itself out.

Mornings start with fifteen minutes of practice: either breathing work (breathing from the belly, holding my breath to get used to higher levels of CO2) or a simple concentration exercise, sitting still and watching my thoughts without following them. I judge it by whether my breath holds and my stillness get longer, not by how calm I feel afterwards.

attention

This is the part most people skip, and I think it decides whether the rest works. Training and sleep don't help much if my attention belongs to my phone.

  • I study six to ten hours a week, in two sessions. My phone is in another room for both.
  • One of the two is done with nothing but the material and my own notes. It shows me what I actually know.
  • Feeds get two fixed twenty-minute slots a day, never anything in between.

What I study in those sessions is on the roadmap.

cadence

Once a week I sit down for twenty minutes and review the week by hand, on paper.

I plan in blocks of twelve weeks. I add one new habit at a time and only start the next once the last one happens without thinking. If a habit fails twice, I take that as a sign it was too big, and I split it into smaller steps.

The whole routine has to be simple enough to write on a single card from memory. If I can't, it has grown too complicated, and something gets cut.

During a block the plan doesn't change. When I come across a new idea or a better system, I write it down and look at it at the end of the block, not in the middle. Rebuilding everything each time something interesting shows up is exactly the habit this is meant to stop.
